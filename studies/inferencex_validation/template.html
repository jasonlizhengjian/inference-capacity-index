How close does real inference get to modeled speed of light?

Kimi K3 is the main comparison across NVIDIA, AMD and Google TPU. GLM-5.2 provides a secondary check with more detailed decode-step logs. The answer depends on whether we compare execution time or an entire serving system.

Three curves, two comparisons. Compare measured InferenceX with our model restricted to submitted deployment layouts; then compare both with the best-known modeled configurations. The default scenario assumes 50% of SOL speed: real TPS = 0.5 × SOL TPS, or real TPOT = 2 × SOL TPOT. This is an assumption, not a fitted hardware efficiency or a change to the index.

What is held fixed in the layout-matched comparison?

Each submitted deployment supplies the decode parallelism, prefill parallelism and allocated pools. We optimize integer batches within its modeled memory limits. Separate P/D pools are limited by the slower of decode capacity and the fixed prefill pool's token budget. Aggregated deployments use an ideal time-sharing resource bound without interference, not a mixed-batch scheduler. Prefill uses PCP1/DCP1 as in the index.

The blue curve includes both the frozen search and these newly evaluated submitted layouts, with the best prefill rate and freely provisioned pools. This ensures it includes the green search space; it is a best-known envelope, not proof of global optimality. The published index itself stays frozen. The 50% discount is applied to both modeled curves consistently, not to measured data.

What does “streaming-equivalent requests” mean?

For each measured run, output throughput per allocated chip is Q, and speed is s = 1 / mean TPOT. We plot Q/s. This expresses throughput in units comparable to concurrent continuous streams, but it is not a count of live requests or agents. Client concurrency, kernel batch and continuously generating requests are different quantities. In particular, reciprocal mean TPOT is not the mean of individual request speeds.

The output-throughput view is a more direct measured quantity. It maximizes output tokens/s/chip among runs meeting each speed floor. Its SOL comparison evaluates each saved configuration at its own native speed, including the modeled prefill-chip requirement.

Agreement at observed points

How closely does the model track the benchmarks?

For each measured frontier point in the selected speed band, compare its throughput or density with the submitted-layout model at the same speed requirement. Take the percentage difference without its sign, then average across points. Each point has equal weight; over- and underprediction do not cancel.

What “submitted-layout” means: the prediction can choose any admitted submitted layout, not necessarily the layout of that measured run. In the five-point Kimi/GB300 example, the modeled winners all use the submitted 16-chip TP16/DCP16 shared-pool layout, with batches 39, 16, 10, 7 and 1. The measured winners use TP8 layouts with different pool allocations. Context, precision and speculative assumptions also remain fixed at the index settings.

Why point errors, step-curve errors and AUC ratios differ

The 12% result compares the five actual output-frontier observations in 50–400, at 60.5, 79.8, 108.8, 143.9 and 387.6 TPS/user. It does not assume measured performance in the large gap between the last two points. Giving every log-speed interval equal weight and using the conservative measured step envelope instead gives about 35% average error. The smoothed orange guide fills those gaps visually, but is not additional measurement and is never used for error calculations.

The measured/model output AUC ratio is 84%, a measure of overall level rather than pointwise accuracy. Capacity has a different frontier and a 77% AUC ratio. Including all nine in-band observations, including dominated runs, gives a 130% average gap from the optimized model; that tests a different question from frontier agreement. We publish both cohorts. The 50% scenario was not fitted to minimize these errors, and the five points do not establish an error bar for other platforms.

02 / Primary model

What the Kimi recipes tell us

Current recipes are substantially more specific than “FP4 Kimi on this chip.” We inspected NVIDIA and AMD startup logs, speculative acceptance counters and running-request samples. These reveal several reasons not to substitute client concurrency into the simulator.

TP16 + DCP16 does not mean 256 chips. In these recipes, decode context parallelism partitions cache across the existing tensor-parallel group. The audited TP16 recipe uses 16 execution chips, not an extra factor of 16. Prefill workers, when separate, add chips to the service denominator.

Two hard implementation limits

Pinned AISim accepts at most five speculative tokens. Several real recipes use seven, so those timings are not replayed or clamped. Its native Kimi/vLLM path also rejects DCP; the supported SGLang path has a different draft graph.

TPU remains a curve comparison

The published Kimi TPU results use FP4 expert storage, while the frozen Ironwood index uses FP8. The serving branch is private and comparable step logs were not obtained. The current TPU curve ratio is therefore neither a precision-matched nor a configuration-matched efficiency estimate.

Approximate Kimi shape probes — why they are not a calibration

For supported speculative widths, the downloadable probes substitute AISim’s SGLang graph and vary sampled running counts, mean/p50/p90 input context, and expert precision. These are timing-only scenarios; memory admission is not checked. Context and batch combinations are not joint observed states. Some ratios exceed 100%: that signals mismatch in the modeled graph or workload, not a physical violation of a correctly specified roofline.

Logged acceptance counters give an execution AL, but recipes can impose synthetic acceptance. They do not establish natural acceptance for AgentX. Selective FP8 overrides in AMD are also not equivalent to a blanket FP8 linear-layer setting.

03 / Secondary model

GLM: a more direct decode-step check

Two GB300 TensorRT-LLM runs expose consecutive iteration records: live scheduled requests, padded verification tokens, cached KV tokens and device time. We pair each iteration’s state with the following record’s previous-step timer.

About 54–74% of SOL speed using live batches; about 66–87% using padded verification shapes. These are two modeling conventions, not a confidence interval. They support plausible high utilization in these configurations—not a universal 50% guarantee, and not a Kimi or TPU calibration.

What is matched, and what remains approximate?

Matched: model family, attention TP/DP, expert parallelism, speculative width three, sampled live batch and cached-context size. Native nextn includes its modeled verification and draft work. We compare raw step times, so accepted length is not used to discount either side.

Remaining approximations: rank-zero logs do not reveal every rank’s batch or context distribution; each observation reduces contexts to their mean. Groups use median context and median device time, weighted by the number of observations. The live-batch convention ignores some padding; the padded convention models extra positions as full requests and can overstate cache work. Engine fusion, graph details and precision exclusions may differ.

The first roughly 26 MB of each large log were sampled. The usable windows cover only the first 14–19 minutes of hour-long profiling runs. Of four inspected GLM logs, one did not reach its profiling window and one had incompatible verification shapes; neither enters the summary. This is not a whole-run unbiased sample.

04 / Reproducible interpretation

Same measurements, two different questions

Upstream accuracy is a separate result. AISim's predecessor, AIConfigurator, reported 7.8% average absolute decode-token latency error in an H100 evaluation using measured kernel data. Our specification-based SOL mode is different. The Kimi comparison here measures service-throughput agreement under a stated 50% efficiency scenario; the GLM check measures achieved speed relative to SOL. These percentages answer different questions.

Step fraction of SOL = modeled step time / observed step time
Measured equivalent density Cᵢ = Qᵢ / sᵢ
At decode efficiency ηD: modeled speed sᵢ → ηD·sᵢ; requests/chip unchanged
Freely provisioned capacity = 1 / [ 1/Cdecode(s/ηD) + r·s/(ηP·P) ]
Fixed P/D pools: min[ Cdecode(s/ηD)·ND/N, ηP·P·NP/(r·s·N) ]
AUC = ∫ C(s) d ln(s) over the selected band,   r = 1.5133425

At 50% decode speed, a 400-SOL-TPS configuration becomes a 200-TPS configuration with the same admitted batch. If prefill also achieves 50%, its throughput is halved too; choose the alternative control to hold it at SOL. With both phases scaled equally, a fixed deployment's output throughput also halves while its streaming-equivalent density at the shifted speed stays unchanged.

Measured AUC uses exact saved breakpoints. The new three-curve model areas use converged numerical integration split at every model speed breakpoint; successive refinements must agree within 1e-6 relatively. This handles crossings between fixed-pool limits. Display smoothing and sampling never enter these areas.

Why the service gap can be larger: real deployments allocate whole pools, carry uneven and changing contexts, pause for tools, perform prefix work and transfer cache. The index instead searches idealized steady-state configurations. Different precision, draft models, acceptance, scheduling and search coverage can move the comparison either way. These data do not identify the contribution of each effect.

What would close the Kimi gap: per-step all-rank scheduled batches and context lengths, exact device timing, actual precision/draft graphs, and native AISim support for the observed vLLM DCP and seven-token recipes. Until then, a precise Kimi “percent of SOL” would claim more than the evidence supports.

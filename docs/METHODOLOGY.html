1. WHAT THE INDEX MEASURES

RESEARCH RATIONALE: WHY THIS COMPARISON?

We want to compare the hardware resources available for serving long-context agents without making today's software implementation the definition of a chip's potential. A measured benchmark captures a particular kernel stack, parallelism strategy and engineering effort, as well as the hardware. Those measurements are indispensable for grounding a model, but unequal software maturity can obscure a hardware comparison. This is especially important when some accelerators are unavailable to us or not yet shipping.

We therefore use an analytical simulator to estimate the resource-implied speed of a specified operation graph. Its compute, memory-transfer and communication costs depend on the hardware specifications, workload and parallelism. This is our operational meaning of SOL: the modeled roofline under those assumptions, rather than an assertion that 100% is achievable. The simulator makes the assumptions reproducible and allows systematic configuration search. It is not inherently the most accurate possible simulator, nor does using a simulator establish the accuracy of its traffic counts or topology model. Our goal is a transparent, comparable analytical baseline, complemented by measurements where available.

The motivating workload is an agent retaining a long conversation or work history, receiving some new input, and generating its next response. We isolate the decode portion because autoregressive generation requires sequential steps with tight per-user latency requirements. Prefill can parallelize computation across many new tokens, often amortizing weight traffic more effectively. This makes decode a particularly useful test of memory bandwidth, resident-cache capacity and scale-up communication. It does NOT establish that decode always dominates total FLOPs, latency or cost: a large new input, expensive long-prefix attention or a strict TTFT target can make prefill important. No input/output token ratio is assumed by this decode-only index.

We fix the existing cache at 262,144 tokens (256 Ki tokens), an empirically informed representative setting for sustained long-context agent workloads. The choice draws on Jason Li's prior workload-distribution analysis, rather than a midpoint between advertised product context limits. His assessment is that roughly 256K is a reasonable average-context proxy, conditional on the harness and its compaction policy. Compaction determines how much of the accumulated history remains resident and therefore materially affects the appropriate representative length.

The associated research is Jason Li, "How many AI agents could run on the AI chips shipped through 2027?" (Epoch AI, 2 October 2026): https://epoch.ai/publications/estimating-the-agent-population with code and data at https://github.com/epoch-research/compute-to-agents. The local TraceLab analysis also explicitly separates the reused prefix from newly appended input and reports their per-step distributions: TraceLab/artifacts/llm_generation/token_length_distribution/README.md and TraceLab/artifacts/llm_generation/prefix_append_distribution/README.md (paths relative to the shared workspace, not this study directory). These sources establish the underlying trace-based workload research; the exact 256Ki choice is the author's representative modeling choice, not a pooled mean independently reconstructed in this methodology update.

The simplification is replacing a distribution of contexts and compaction states with one fixed context for every concurrent decoding request. This keeps the hardware comparison controlled and tractable; it does not imply that every request has that length or that performance at the average context equals average performance over a trace. Memory admission and configuration changes can make that relationship nonlinear. Shorter-context and million-token workloads remain separate sensitivities; their cache capacity and attention costs could change relative results.

There is no single universally appropriate generation speed. Throughput- oriented rollouts and users tolerant of slower output may accept a lower speed, whereas interactive or premium experiences can value faster output. We therefore compare the maximum concurrent decoding requests at many speed floors, not only one batch size or one latency target. The current band is 50–400 SOL TPS/user. If a configuration achieved 50% of its modeled SOL speed, this would correspond to 25–200 actual TPS/user. The conversion is illustrative: the headline contains no performance discount.

Our empirical grounding makes roughly 50% of modeled SOL a useful planning scenario in some configurations, not a universal demonstrated floor. In the earlier matched GLM analysis, reuse-aware estimates imply about 45–52%; the retained DeepSeek examples were nearer 30–38%. Those studies use a different AIC/backend path and do not calibrate every current AISim curve. Actual efficiency varies with model, precision, software, batch, topology and hardware. Measurements or calibrated lower-level models would be needed to estimate those differences; the present index does not claim to know them. The saved evidence is in the sibling experiments inferencex-agentx-grounding (output/MATCHED_DECODE_CALIBRATION.html and matched_decode_summary.csv) and glm-attention-dimension-study (output/native_recalibration.json). The first report explicitly marks its original, higher GLM ratios as historical.

We summarize each capacity frontier with area under the curve so the index reflects a range of acceptable interactivities. Logarithmic integration gives equal horizontal weight to each doubling of required speed. It avoids allowing equal absolute TPS increments to give the fastest doubling most of the horizontal weight, while retaining an explicit high-speed boundary. This is a chosen evaluation measure, not an estimated market-demand model. The full curves and linear-weighting sensitivity remain important because one number can conceal where each accelerator's advantage occurs.

We leave the ultra-fast regime, such as 1,000 actual TPS/user, outside this headline and potentially in a separate future track. That is a scope choice: very tight latency limits change the feasible configurations and can favor specialized designs. On any one saved hardware/model frontier, raising the speed floor cannot increase maximum agents/chip, although it need not lower aggregate token throughput. We have not established that ultra-fast demand is small, that only specialized accelerators can meet it, or that the chosen band represents most current demand. Faster serial reasoning could have substantial value even when the served concurrent population is smaller.

Finally, we use two concrete large open-model architectures, GLM-5.2 and Kimi-K3, rather than guessing proprietary model internals. Their attention designs are materially different: GLM uses sparse DSA, while the larger Kimi combines MLA with recurrent KDA layers. They provide useful, implementable anchors for the comparison. Popularity is a motivation, not a measured selection criterion. More models or larger hypothetical architectures could broaden coverage, but would add scenario and validation work, and possibly more expensive serving cases. We do not assume they would leave rankings unchanged: size, sparsity, depth, cache representation and deployment fit can all shift the hardware advantage. Equal weighting of these two models is an intentionally simple starting portfolio, not architecture completeness.

FORMAL OBJECTIVE

The question is: how many simultaneous long-context decoding requests can an accelerator support per allocated physical chip while meeting a specified per-user generation-speed floor?

For each hardware/model pair, we build a capacity-versus-speed frontier. We integrate that frontier over 50–400 modeled SOL tokens/second/user, normalize its area to the same model on GB300, then take the arithmetic mean of the GLM and Kimi scores.

A score of 2 means twice GB300's normalized capacity-area on this two-model, fixed-workload index. It does NOT mean twice the capacity at every speed, twice the speed at fixed concurrency, twice the throughput of every workload, twice the real production performance, or twice the performance per watt.

This is a per-allocated-chip resource-efficiency index. It is not a per-rack, per-dollar, per-watt, or equal-quality index. A physical chip is not an equal-sized economic or silicon unit across vendors. Read it alongside the memory, bandwidth, precision, interconnect, and deployment-policy columns.

“Agent” here means one resident decoding request. Tool-wait residency, prefill, queueing, network service overhead, tool execution, and the agent's success rate are not included. The index is a proxy for the inference component of agent capacity, not completed tasks per unit time.

2. WORKLOAD AND MODEL SCOPE

Every request has 262,144 existing cached tokens (256 Ki tokens). We model steady-state decode at that context, not processing the prefix anew. This is a fixed-context snapshot, not the average of an entire growing sequence. No new-input prefill work or cached-prefix prefill is mixed into the index.

The headline includes only GLM-5.2 and Kimi-K3, with 50% weight each. These are the saved native model representations in our study. The label “real model” distinguishes them from hypothetical architectures; it does not imply the simulator reproduces every implementation detail accurately.

The earlier 5T/10T hypotheses, SWA studies, depth sweeps, Qwen experiments, and architecture ensembles remain useful separate sensitivities. They do not receive headline weight. Two models are a deliberately limited sample: they do not cover every plausible attention architecture or proprietary frontier model, nor do they establish equal model quality across precisions.

All requests within a candidate use the same workload shape. The current native MoE SOL formula balances routed assignments across ranks and caps expert weight reads at the resident expert inventory. It does not calculate probabilistic expected overlap between requests; below saturation it behaves like unique expert reads up to that cap. This is not a trace-driven test of correlated routing, rank hot spots, or uneven live request counts. The concurrency is balanced by the modeled configuration, not demonstrated to remain balanced under production traffic.

3. SPECULATIVE DECODING AND PRECISION

Speculative decoding IS included. The saved setting is nextn = 3, giving four verification positions, with an assumed average accepted output length AL = 3 per modeled iteration. AL is accepted tokens per iteration, not a scalar 75% per-draft-token acceptance probability; one cannot recover the full acceptance distribution from AL alone.

The scoring code uses:

    raw_iteration_ms = sum(native operation latencies) + applicable overlay
    TPOT_ms = raw_iteration_ms / 3
    TPS_per_user = 3000 / raw_iteration_ms

The raw native nextn graph is the timing source. It does include draft work: GLM approximates MTP overhead by scaling target-layer work by (L+3)/L; Kimi adds explicit five-layer DSPARK draft operations. We do not add a separately measured production draft-server time. This should not be described as a validated full speculative-serving implementation. Verification/cache reuse is imperfectly represented; our retained assumptions can overcharge cache reads. Fixed AL across hardware, quantization, batches and models is an assumption, not a measured guarantee. It can affect relative results too.

Expert weights/compute use NVFP4 W4A4 or a hardware-adapted FP4-equivalent proxy, with nominal modeled storage 0.5625 bytes/weight. Exceptions are H100, H200 and TPU v7 Ironwood: their headline uses FP8 experts, nominally 1 byte/weight, with no invented native FP4 compute rate.

Other GEMMs and FMHA are configured as BF16, KV as FP8, and communication as half precision. Actual native operator choices have exceptions: Kimi's MLA decode attention uses the FP8 compute ceiling when FP8 KV and the configured SM capability select it. GLM DSA uses BF16 attention and an FP8 indexer. GLM cache accounting also retains BF16 rotary values and indexer scale bytes; “FP8 KV” is not one byte for every stored state element. These settings are not a claim about every production kernel. There is no additional structured-sparsity compute multiplier. Cross-format accuracy equivalence has not been established. The comparison asks what hardware-adapted serving could achieve under these resource assumptions, not which hardware runs an identical quantized checkpoint fastest at identical accuracy.

4. WHAT “SOL” MEANS HERE

We use AISim's operation-level SOL mode with performance corrections disabled and non-SOL fallbacks denied by configuration. Native operations estimate resource costs from model shape, batch, precision, parallelism, hardware compute rates, HBM bandwidth and collective transfer rates. The study sums the emitted operation latencies and applies an explicit topology overlay where specified. This is not one whole-model FLOP-count division.

It is a conditional operation-graph/resource estimate, not a rigorous global lower bound on execution time. Imperfect reuse assumptions may overcount traffic; omitted computation or contention may undercount it. Different graph schedules, kernel fusion, SRAM residency and overlap can change the physical lower bound. Therefore a real result exceeding this particular model's estimate would identify a modeling mismatch, not violate physics.

No simulator modifications, hardware peak changes, or new timing runs are part of the 50–400 update. We re-integrate existing policy-filtered curves. This update does not use sglang-sim scheduling results or an InferenceX serving trace to generate the cross-hardware frontier.

Known model-path limitations include GLM's full-indexer approximation and Kimi's memory-only recurrent timing without a corresponding arithmetic ceiling. These errors need not cancel between accelerators with different compute/bandwidth ratios. Earlier empirical grounding is useful evidence for selected configurations, not a universal calibration of all vendors, all parallelisms, all precision choices, or the 256K workload.

5. MEMORY ADMISSION

A candidate must fit the native memory budget before it can enter a curve. The configured fraction is 90% of device capacity. Native weights, workspace and fixed memory reservations are estimated, but admission is backend-specific: vLLM subtracts transient activations from the KV budget, whereas SGLang leaves them outside its static pool. Displayed memory_gib includes those diagnostic activation bytes, so it is not universally constrained to 90% of capacity. Cache is reserved for the existing context plus four verification positions. An additional capacity guard of (weights + KV)/32 covers unmodeled metadata conservatively; it is not a correction applied to the timing estimates.

PP candidates have additional unequal-stage weight and boundary-buffer guards. The retained serial PP policy reserves whole-model KV on each stage, which is conservative and can understate PP capacity. This is not an optimized stage-local cache implementation.

“Memory full” means the maximum admitted integer batch under this policy, not 100% of nominal HBM bytes. Decimal GB and binary GiB must not be mixed: 1 GiB = 2^30 bytes. The CSV records both. Fixed memory settings and native workspaces mean the admitted capacity cannot be reconstructed from nominal device memory minus weights alone.

6. PARALLELISM, CHIP COUNTS AND DEPLOYMENT POLICY

Each curve selects the best admissible tested instance and integer batch for the speed floor. The chip-budget column is a maximum searched instance size, not a promise that every winning point uses every available chip. Changing the speed floor may change batch, layout, or instance size.

For ordinary PP1 rows:

    physical_chips = TP × attention_DP = expert_TP × expert_EP
    requests = batch_per_attention_replica × attention_DP
    agents_per_chip = requests / physical_chips

DCP subdivides the attention TP group; it is not another multiplier on physical chips. For example, TP8 DP8 DCP8 uses 64 chips, not 512. Its KV partitioning and attention communication still matter, even if TP and DCP have the same numerical size. Attention and expert partitions are distinct descriptions of the same physical allocation, not two separate deployments.

For serial PP, all stages count in the denominator, including waiting stages. The saved “world” field already includes PP; multiplying it by PP again would double-count allocated chips. There is no throughput × PP bonus.

Budget ceilings in the headline are 72 chips for GB200, GB300, VR200 and MI455X; 128 for Jalapeno, TPU v7/v8t/v8i and the four Huawei profiles; and 64 for B200, B300, MI355X, H100 and H200. These are search ceilings, not claims about maximum physically possible systems or equal rack sizes. Expert divisibility and the tested layouts can prevent use of a full budget.

B200/B300/MI355X/H100/H200 restrict TP, EP and DCP to an eight-chip local domain. Larger instances require the tested serial PP schedule. This is a policy-conditioned comparison: it intentionally avoids granting those platforms frictionless cross-node expert parallelism, but it also does not find their best possible interleaved PP implementation. Treating their scores as an unconditional architectural maximum would be misleading.

Large-domain platforms use the admitted PP1 layouts with their respective fabric assumptions. Integer expert placement is required. Layout search is finite, not an exhaustive proof of global optimality. The archived candidate enumerations are authoritative; a nominal 72- or 128-chip budget alone does not tell you which layouts were actually feasible.

Unused budget chips are excluded. If a 16-chip instance wins under a 72-chip ceiling, four copies would allocate 64 chips; the eight leftover chips do not depress its score. By contrast, a waiting stage inside one allocated PP instance is not an unused chip and must remain counted.

For independent instances, a mixture's requests/allocated-chip ratio is a chip-weighted average of its component densities. It cannot exceed the best component density. That justifies optimizing this metric over single instances. It does not solve maximum total agents on a fully purchased rack, integer rack-packing efficiency, or contention between replicated instances.

7. FROM CANDIDATES TO A CAPACITY FRONTIER

Let q denote a tested configuration/batch, n(q) its resident requests, g(q) its allocated physical chips, and v(q) its modeled TPS/user. For model m and hardware h, at a required speed s define:

    C[h,m](s) = max { n(q)/g(q) : q admitted and v(q) >= s }

If no candidate meets s, capacity is zero. It is not a missing observation, and the unsupported part of the band must not be dropped from the integral. Equality qualifies: a candidate at exactly 100 TPS meets a 100-TPS floor.

C(s) is a nonincreasing staircase. Its flat sections and drops reflect integer batch sizes, finite layout choices, memory limits and speed limits. They are not evidence that physical latency is discontinuous. Lines drawn between points must not invent fractional batches or extrapolate capacity beyond the fastest tested candidate.

The frontier is an envelope of alternative deployments. A point chosen for 50 TPS and a point chosen for 400 TPS do not necessarily coexist on one fixed deployment. AUC is a summary across speed requirements, not a promise that a single configuration achieves the entire curve simultaneously.

The actual throughput of a winning point is n(q)/g(q) × v(q). Multiplying capacity by the requested floor s gives only the throughput guaranteed by that floor. Neither quantity is the capacity AUC used in the headline.

8. THE EXACT INDEX EQUATIONS

The current band is L = 50 and U = 400 SOL TPS/user. Define:

    A[h,m] = integral from L to U of C[h,m](s) d ln(s)
           = integral from L to U of C[h,m](s) ds/s
    R[h,m] = A[h,m] / A[GB300,m]
    Index[h] = 0.5 × R[h,GLM] + 0.5 × R[h,Kimi]

Normalize each model FIRST, then average. Pooling raw areas before normalizing would implicitly give greater model weight to whichever model has higher GB300 capacity. That is not our chosen equal-model index.

This is a ratio of areas followed by an ARITHMETIC mean. It is not a geometric mean, not the mean of the 50/100/200/400 point ratios, and not the log-uniform mean of pointwise hardware/GB300 ratios.

Dividing every raw area by ln(U/L) produces a mean capacity over log speed. That common factor cancels in R. Natural logarithm versus base-2 logarithm also cancels in the score; the logarithm base does not affect rankings.

Exact integration uses the sorted frontier breakpoints inside the band, plus L and U. On each open interval (a,b), capacity is constant, so its contribution is C(midpoint) × ln(b/a). Sum the interval contributions. Endpoint conventions have zero effect on the area. No trapezoidal interpolation or arbitrary sample density is needed.

GB300 normalizes to exactly 1 for both models and their mean. If a future model has zero GB300 area throughout the chosen band, its normalized score is undefined, not zero or infinity; it needs a new reference or a revised metric. If GB300 has zero capacity over only part of the band, a ratio of areas can still be defined, but pointwise ratio plots need explicit masking.

9. WHY LOG SPEED, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT IMPLY

The logarithm is a choice of evaluation measure, not a discovery about the distribution of real users. Calling it “log-uniform” means equal weight per equal multiplicative interval of required speed. We have not established that production speed requirements are distributed log-uniformly.

From 50 to 400, the intervals 50–100, 100–200 and 200–400 each span one doubling. They therefore each receive one-third of horizontal log measure. Linear weighting over the same band gives them 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7 of the horizontal measure (14.3%, 28.6%, 57.1%). Linear weighting emphasizes equal absolute TPS increments; logarithmic weighting emphasizes equal proportional speed improvements. Neither is uniquely correct without a demand/utility model. Both the measure AND the chosen band are policy choices.

IMPORTANT: equal horizontal measure does not mean equal score contribution. There are generally more agents/chip at slower speeds, so the low-speed interval can contribute more capacity area even with logarithmic weighting.

When GB300 capacity is positive throughout the band, another useful identity is:

    R[h,m] = integral r(s) × w(s) d ln(s)
    r(s) = C[h,m](s) / C[GB300,m](s)
    w(s) = C[GB300,m](s) / A[GB300,m]

Thus the ratio of capacity areas is a baseline-capacity-weighted average of relative advantage, not an equal-log-speed-weighted average of that advantage. This is intentional if the quantity of interest is accumulated agent capacity. If the desired quantity were relative advantage at a randomly selected log speed, averaging ratios would be a different index.

The 50–400 band retains slower agents relevant to throughput-oriented or RL uses, while extending through a meaningfully fast regime. It is not a coverage claim about all workloads. An eventual ultra-fast 200–1000 track could be separate. It should not be silently substituted into this index.

Suggested publication wording:

“We summarize modeled long-context decode capacity using area under the maximum agents-per-allocated-chip frontier over 50–400 SOL tokens/s/user. Integration is logarithmic in speed, giving equal horizontal weight to each doubling of the speed requirement. We normalize each model's area to GB300 before taking an equal-weight arithmetic mean across GLM and Kimi. This is a transparent workload-weighting convention, not an empirically estimated demand distribution; linear-weighting sensitivities are reported.”

Do not choose weights to force B300 to 0.5 or Rubin to 2. Such expectations are sanity checks, not calibration targets. A score should remain traceable to the workload, admissible configurations and stated weighting rule.

10. SOL VERSUS ACHIEVABLE PERFORMANCE

The headline is pure modeled SOL. No 0.5× or 0.7× speed discount is applied. Those factors are scenarios, not validated per-hardware calibrations.

If, at every fixed configuration and concurrency, achieved speed were eta × modeled speed, the achieved capacity at floor s would be C_SOL(s/eta). At eta = 0.5, our 50–400 SOL band corresponds to 25–200 achieved TPS/user. To assess 50–400 achieved TPS at eta = 0.5 instead, re-integrate the SOL frontiers over 100–800, including zeros where they cannot meet the target.

Halving speed doubles TPOT. “0.5× SOL latency” would mean faster, not slower; use “50% of modeled SOL speed” to avoid that ambiguity.

A common efficiency multiplier does not generally preserve the relative index at a fixed achieved-speed band: shifting the band crosses different memory plateaus and latency-limited regions. Vendor-specific efficiency, precision-specific kernels, and layout-dependent communication can change the ratios further. The current index is not a real-performance forecast with a rigorously established confidence interval.

11. CONSEQUENTIAL CROSS-HARDWARE UNCERTAINTIES

All hardware numbers in the accompanying CSV are the frozen simulation inputs. They are not newly verified shipping specifications. Blank fields mean unknown/not applicable, not zero. The resource abstraction transfers an NVIDIA-oriented operation graph; it is not a native compiler/kernel simulation for each vendor.

NVIDIA and AMD large scale-up domains: the saved peak bandwidths abstract collective execution. Switch contention, placement, protocol efficiency and startup effects are not comprehensively calibrated. FP4 support and peak rates alone do not validate the same operation schedule on every chip.

Eight-chip platforms: the node-local/serial-PP restriction avoids implausibly cheap cross-node EP, but serial PP and conservative cache admission can understate the capability of a well-tuned pipeline. Scores are conditional on this policy. This asymmetry must stay visible when comparing them with large scale-up domains.

Jalapeno: BF16 throughput is assumed to be half FP8. The saved fabric input is 600 GB/s treated as one-way, with unresolved directionality. Capacity is 216 GiB, not 216 decimal GB. Its score is conditional on these choices.

TPU v7/v8t: aggregate outbound ICI bandwidth is an injection bound, not a torus routing/congestion simulation. The simulator's node-size placeholder is not a fully connected physical switch domain. Chiplet/internal memory domains, forwarding and placement constraints are omitted. Ironwood uses FP8 experts; v8t uses the agreed FP4-equivalent adaptation assumption.

TPU v8i: the saved corrected compute rates and decimal-GB memory input are used. The topology overlay assumes 250 ns/hop with 1/3/7-hop tiers at <=4 / <=32 / >32 chips and recursive collective accounting. This is a sensitivity abstraction, not a calibrated Boardfly implementation. A local four-chip block does not imply that useful scale-up stops at four chips.

Huawei: the headline selects the BASE resource case under a 128-chip search ceiling, not an average of uncertainty scenarios. 950 inputs derive from the saved whitepaper profiles; 960 inputs are roadmap assumptions. Fabric peak directionality, port allocation/optics, collective routing and dense versus sparse peak conventions require care. BF16 on 960 is inferred as half FP8. The FP4 byte model is a proxy for the hardware-native format. No matched 256K GLM/Kimi Ascend serving measurements calibrate these curves. Half-link and other saved cases are assumption sensitivities, not confidence bounds.

Source tags such as “silicon” or “mixed” are not on their own proof of measured timing: an audited dispatch wrapper can emit such a tag around underlying analytical collectives. Evidence status must follow the actual operator path, not the label alone.

Large SRAM may improve actual execution or alter the relevant traffic bound. The current abstraction does not explicitly model persistent SRAM residency, tiling and reuse across all operations. A universal percent-of-SOL discount cannot rigorously correct that omission or arbitrary topology differences.

Additional source-audit caveats, not changes to the saved scores: the legacy nextn/MTP memory path does not add separate draft-weight residency, and Kimi's explicit generation-only draft operations are absent from the base context-weight inventory. Kimi DCP timing leaves draft attention unsharded, while its memory helper divides combined target-plus-draft token-cache bytes by DCP. These are accounting inconsistencies whose numerical effects have not been quantified in this index. The relevant AISim source is under repos/aisimulate-oct05/python/aisimulate/src/aisimulate_core/sdk/: speculation/mtp.py (draft_weights_bytes), models/base.py (get_resident_weights_bytes and _apply_decode_context_parallel), and models/kimi_k3.py (get_kvcache_rank_bytes_per_sequence). The analytical approach remains useful; these caveats delimit what the current implementation actually supports, rather than implying that the scores have been corrected.

These are computational consistency checks, not empirical validation of AISim.

13. INSTRUCTIONS FOR A GRAPHICS AGENT

Use the supplied data, not numbers transcribed from rounded prose. Keep the headline labeled “50–400 SOL TPS/user · log AUC · GLM/Kimi 50:50”. Do not add hypothetical models, infer power-normalized values, or silently apply an achievable-performance discount.

Suggested figure 1: the five-step calculation. Tested configurations -> capacity frontier -> log-speed area -> per-model GB300 normalization -> equal arithmetic mean. Show both raw areas and the dimensionless ratios.

Suggested figure 2: GB300 and one comparator's actual step frontiers for each model. X = minimum SOL TPS/user on a log axis, 50–400; Y = maximum resident requests per allocated chip. Use identical axes within a pair, zero-baseline Y axes, and explicit solid/dashed labels if styles differ. Shade each raw area; display their ratio. Do not smooth staircase corners.

Suggested figure 3: three doubling intervals with equal widths on a log axis versus widths 1:2:4 on a linear axis. Separately show actual area contributions from band_contributions.csv. Caption the distinction between horizontal weighting and capacity-weighted contributions.

Suggested figure 4: two per-model score bars feeding the mean. Use MI455X, VR200 or another real row. Do not visually average raw capacities instead.

Suggested figure 5: current log50–400 versus linear50–400, with historical log50–300 and linear50–200 as clearly labeled sensitivities. Rank/order changes need numerical annotations; avoid truncating axes to exaggerate small changes. The headline remains log50–400 in every panel.

Suggested figure 6: a winning-layout strip beneath a frontier. Label TP, DP, DCP, EP, ETP, PP, batch/replica and allocated chips at selected speed floors. Distinguish instance allocation from maximum chip search budget.

Show unsupported high-speed regions as zero capacity rather than gaps or missing data. At a breakpoint, the old capacity still qualifies exactly; immediately above it, the next feasible step applies. Decimal agents/chip are a ratio of integer requests to chips, not fractional live requests.

Every graphic should say “modeled, not measured” and identify context, speculation and precision exceptions. Give topology/PP caveats a visible legend or caption, not only a buried footnote. Keep hardware-rate figures labeled as frozen model inputs. The curves compare supported modeled operating choices; they do not establish a trained model's quality.
